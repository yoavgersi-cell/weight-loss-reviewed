The short version

Wegovy and Zepbound are the two FDA-approved GLP-1 injections for weight loss, and in the only head-to-head trial — SURMOUNT-5 — Zepbound (tirzepatide) won. Over 72 weeks, people on Zepbound lost about 20.2% of their body weight versus 13.7% on Wegovy (semaglutide), and had fewer people stop over side effects. That's a real, roughly 6–7 percentage-point edge. But "better on average" is not "better for everyone." Wegovy has the longer track record and a landmark trial showing it cuts heart-attack and stroke risk; a new higher 7.2 mg Wegovy dose (approved 2026) narrows the weight-loss gap; and cost, insurance coverage, side-effect tolerance and what your prescriber recommends often decide the choice more than a trial average does. This guide lays out the real numbers side by side — results, side effects, dosing, and 2026 pricing — then shows exactly how to get either one online, including the lower-cost compounded route and its trade-offs. What Wegovy and Zepbound actually are

Start with the basics, because a lot of confusion comes from mixing up brand names and molecules. Wegovy is Novo Nordisk's brand name for semaglutide dosed for weight loss. Zepbound is Eli Lilly's brand name for tirzepatide dosed for weight loss. Same molecules you've heard of under other names: semaglutide is also sold as Ozempic (for type 2 diabetes) and as an oral pill; tirzepatide is also sold as Mounjaro (for diabetes). When people argue "Ozempic vs Mounjaro," they're really arguing semaglutide vs tirzepatide — the same contest as Wegovy vs Zepbound, just under the diabetes labels.

Both drugs belong to a class called incretin mimics — they copy gut hormones your body releases after you eat. Those hormones do several things that add up to eating less without white-knuckle willpower: they slow how fast your stomach empties (so you feel full longer), they act on appetite centers in the brain (so you're less hungry and less preoccupied with food), and they improve how your body handles blood sugar and insulin. The Harvard Health overview walks through the underlying biology in plain terms. The National Institutes of Health's NIDDK lists both among the prescription medications approved to treat obesity.

Here's the one mechanistic difference that explains most of the rest of this guide. Wegovy (semaglutide) targets one receptor: GLP-1. Zepbound (tirzepatide) targets two: GLP-1 and GIP. Tirzepatide is a "dual agonist" — it mimics a second gut hormone, GIP, on top of the GLP-1 action semaglutide has. The leading explanation for why Zepbound tends to produce more weight loss is exactly this: it's pulling two metabolic levers instead of one. That's a simplification — the biology of GIP in obesity is genuinely complicated and still being worked out — but as a mental model, "one hormone vs two" is the right starting intuition for why the trials shook out the way they did.

Both are FDA-approved specifically for chronic weight management, which matters more than it sounds. Approval means each went through large trials for this exact use and carries an approved label, a known dosing schedule, manufacturer quality control, and a defined safety profile. Wegovy was approved for weight management in 2021; Zepbound followed with its own weight-management approval in 2023. You can read the plain-language drug information for each at MedlinePlus — semaglutide and tirzepatide. Keep that approved-for-this-use status in mind, because it's the single biggest thing separating these two brands from the compounded versions many online programs sell cheaply — a distinction we return to in detail later.

Both are, in their primary form, once-weekly injections you give yourself with a pre-filled pen or a vial and syringe, in the fat just under the skin of the belly, thigh, or upper arm. The needles are short and fine; most people describe the shot as a minor pinch, and the weekly rhythm is easy to build a habit around. There's one important wrinkle to the "injection" framing: semaglutide now also comes as an oral pill (the Wegovy pill, a 25 mg tablet approved in late 2025), which changes the convenience calculus for people who dread needles. We cover the pill where it's relevant below and in depth in our dedicated GLP-1 weight-loss pill guide; for the core Wegovy-vs-Zepbound comparison, though, we're talking about the two injections, since that's what the head-to-head trial tested and what most people start on.

One last bit of grounding: these are not interchangeable with "Ozempic for weight loss" in a regulatory sense, even though the molecule is the same. Wegovy and Zepbound are the versions dosed and labeled for weight management; prescribing a diabetes brand for weight loss is an off-label decision. For the purposes of this guide — choosing a weight-loss treatment and getting it legitimately — Wegovy and Zepbound are the on-label options, and that's what we compare.

The head-to-head trial: SURMOUNT-5

Almost every "X vs Y" drug comparison online is really a cross-trial comparison — someone takes the weight-loss number from one drug's study and the number from a different drug's study and lines them up. That's genuinely unreliable, because the two trials enrolled different people, ran for different lengths, used different diet-and-exercise backdrops, and measured things slightly differently. The gold standard is a trial that gives the same people one drug or the other and compares them directly. For Wegovy vs Zepbound, that trial exists, and it's the most important single piece of evidence in this entire guide.

It's called SURMOUNT-5, and it was published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2025 (Aronne et al., NEJM 2025). Here's the design in plain terms: 751 adults with obesity but without diabetes were randomly assigned to take either tirzepatide (Zepbound) or semaglutide (Wegovy), each pushed to the maximum dose the person could tolerate, for 72 weeks — about 16 months. Both groups were titrated up the normal way and supported with lifestyle guidance. Then the researchers measured who lost more weight. Because it's randomized and head-to-head, it sidesteps the apples-to-oranges problem that plagues every cross-trial claim.

The result was clear: Zepbound produced more weight loss than Wegovy. On average, the tirzepatide group lost about 20.2% of their body weight, versus about 13.7% in the semaglutide group. In absolute terms that's roughly a 22.8 kg (about 50 lb) average loss on Zepbound versus about 15.0 kg (about 33 lb) on Wegovy — a gap of around 6.5 percentage points of body weight, or stated as a relative figure the way the headlines did, roughly 47% more mean weight loss on tirzepatide. The trial also tracked waist circumference, where tirzepatide again came out further ahead. By the measure most people care about, this is as close to a definitive answer as drug research gives you: on average, Zepbound takes off more.

Now the honest caveats, because they matter and because a trial is only as good as its fine print. SURMOUNT-5 was open-label — participants knew which drug they were on — which is a real limitation, though for an objective outcome like weight it's a modest one. It enrolled people without diabetes, so it doesn't directly speak to people with type 2 diabetes, in whom GLP-1 weight responses tend to run lower. It's a single trial; one study, however well run, is one study. And crucially, it compared the drugs at the doses that existed when it was designed — which, as we'll see in a moment, is about to be complicated by a new, higher Wegovy dose that wasn't in this trial. None of these caveats erase the result. They right-size it: a clear average advantage for Zepbound, from one strong randomized trial, in people without diabetes, at the then-standard doses.

It's also worth being clear about why a single trial carries this much weight, since "one study" is sometimes used to wave results away. SURMOUNT-5 is persuasive not because one study is ever the last word, but because it's the right design for this exact question — randomized, head-to-head, adequately sized, long enough to capture the full weight-loss arc, and pointing in the same direction as each drug's separate pivotal trial. When a direct comparison and both drugs' individual trials all agree that tirzepatide loses more, that's a consistent body of evidence, not a lone outlier. The appropriate humility is about the size of the gap in any given person and about the populations not studied — not about the direction, which is about as settled as this kind of question gets.

The most important thing SURMOUNT-5 does is retire the lazy claim that "they're basically the same." On average, they're not — Zepbound loses more. But the trial measures an average, and the single biggest mistake people make with this number is treating it as a personal prediction. Averages hide enormous spread: in these trials, some people on Wegovy lost more than the average Zepbound user, and some people on Zepbound lost little. Which brings us to the part the head-to-head number can't tell you — how much you might lose, and everything else that goes into the choice.

Weight loss: how much on each, realistically

The head-to-head number answers "which loses more on average." It does not answer "how much will I lose," and conflating the two is the source of a lot of disappointment and a lot of overspending. Let's build the realistic picture, using each drug's own pivotal trial alongside the head-to-head.

For Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4 mg), the landmark trial was STEP 1 (NEJM 2021), where adults with obesity lost on average about 14.9% of body weight over 68 weeks. For Zepbound (tirzepatide), the pivotal trial was SURMOUNT-1 (NEJM 2022), where the highest dose produced an average of roughly 21% of body weight lost over 72 weeks. Those two trials, plus the head-to-head, all point the same direction: Wegovy tends to land in the mid-teens percentage-wise, Zepbound in the high-teens to around 20%. Our dedicated breakdown of how much weight you can realistically lose on a GLP-1 digs into the distribution behind these averages.

But averages are the least useful number for an individual. Here's what the spread actually looks like, and it's the part the ads skip. In these trials, weight loss ranged from people who lost almost nothing to people who lost 30% or more. Roughly speaking, on semaglutide about a third of people lost 15% or more of their weight; on tirzepatide, a substantially larger share hit that mark, and a meaningful minority lost 25% or more. The takeaway isn't a single number to expect — it's a range to plan for, with the odds of a big result better on Zepbound but a genuinely good result very achievable on either. Two people on the same drug at the same dose can have completely different outcomes, driven by biology no questionnaire can predict in advance.

Then there's the twist that complicates the clean "Zepbound wins" story: the new, higher-dose Wegovy. In 2026 the FDA approved Wegovy HD, a 7.2 mg semaglutide dose (Novo Nordisk, 2026), above the previous 2.4 mg ceiling. In its trial program, the higher dose pushed average weight loss up toward roughly 18–21% depending on how it's measured — meaningfully more than the 2.4 mg dose that lost to Zepbound in SURMOUNT-5. The honest framing: this narrows the gap, and may close much of it for some people — but the head-to-head trial used the old 2.4 mg dose, so there is no direct comparison of Zepbound against 7.2 mg Wegovy yet. Anyone telling you the new dose definitively ties or beats Zepbound is guessing ahead of the evidence. What we can say: the higher dose makes Wegovy a stronger contender than the 13.7% head-to-head figure alone suggests, at the cost of more side effects at the top dose.

Two realities sit underneath all of these numbers and matter more than which drug you pick. First, the weight loss is dose-dependent and titration-dependent — people who rush the dose or never reach an effective one get less, and people who can't tolerate the top dose land lower than the trial average regardless of brand. Second, and this is the one nobody wants to hear, the results depend on staying on the drug. These are treatments for a chronic condition, not a course of antibiotics; when people stop, weight tends to come back. In the semaglutide withdrawal data, people regained a large share of what they'd lost within about a year of stopping — our guide on what happens when you stop a GLP-1 covers this in full. Whichever brand loses you the most weight is the wrong question if you can't afford or sustain it long-term; the drug you can actually stay on usually beats the drug with the better trial average you quit in month four.

It helps to think in responder tiers rather than a single average, because that's how the results actually distribute. Roughly: a minority of people are "super-responders" who lose 25% or more and are often astonished by the result; a large middle group lands in the clinically meaningful 10–20% range that improves health markers substantially; a smaller group gets a modest 5–10%; and a minority are "low responders" who, despite doing everything right, lose relatively little. Zepbound shifts more people up into the higher tiers than Wegovy does, which is what the better average reflects — but every tier contains users of both drugs. The uncomfortable truth no program advertises is that you won't know your tier until you try, because the biology that determines it isn't something a questionnaire can read in advance. This is also why a fair trial matters: writing a drug off before a maintenance dose risks mislabeling yourself a low responder when you simply hadn't reached an effective dose yet.

So, realistically: expect a range, not a guarantee; tilt the odds of a larger result toward Zepbound, especially against standard-dose Wegovy; give whichever drug you choose a real titration and several months before judging it; and weigh the trial averages against the far more decision-relevant question of which one you can sustain. The "best" weight-loss drug is the one that produces a good-enough result you can keep.

Side effects: how the two compare

Weight loss is only half the ledger. The other half — the one that actually drives most people's day-to-day experience and a lot of the quitting — is side effects. The good news is that the two drugs have broadly similar side-effect profiles, because they work through overlapping mechanisms. The nuances between them are real but smaller than the marketing on either side implies.

For both drugs, the side effects are overwhelmingly gastrointestinal: nausea, constipation, diarrhea, vomiting, and reflux or that "food is just sitting there" fullness. They come from the same place for both — the drugs slow your stomach — and they follow the same pattern for both: worst when you start and each time you step up the dose, then fading as your body adapts. In the pivotal trials, nausea was the most common complaint, affecting a large share of people on semaglutide and a somewhat smaller share on tirzepatide, with the other GI effects trailing behind. Our full GLP-1 side-effects guide breaks down each one and exactly how to manage it; the short version is that these are common, usually mild-to-moderate, and largely controllable with slow titration and simple food-and-timing habits.

Does one drug cause fewer side effects? This is where the head-to-head trial is quietly illuminating. Despite producing more weight loss, Zepbound in SURMOUNT-5 had a lower rate of people stopping over GI side effects — about 2.7% discontinued for that reason on tirzepatide versus about 5.6% on semaglutide. That's a useful data point against the intuition that "stronger drug = rougher ride." It doesn't mean Zepbound is side-effect-free or gentle for everyone — plenty of people get nausea on it — but at least in that trial, the more effective drug was also the one fewer people quit over. Tolerance is deeply individual, though: some people breeze through semaglutide and struggle on tirzepatide, and the only way to know your own response is to try, under supervision, with a careful titration.

Beyond the common GI stuff, both drugs share the same tier of rarer but serious risks, and they're the reason these belong with a real prescriber rather than a checkout form. Both carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors (based on rodent studies) and are not for people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2. Both can raise the risk of gallbladder problems (rapid weight loss does this independently) and, rarely, pancreatitis. Both can cause low blood sugar when combined with certain diabetes medications like insulin or sulfonylureas. These risks are uncommon, but they're real, they're class-wide, and they don't meaningfully separate the two drugs — which is exactly why "which has fewer side effects" is less decisive than people expect. The serious-risk column is essentially a tie; the common-GI column slightly favors Zepbound on the head-to-head dropout data.

A practical note that applies to both: a large majority of the side-effect difference you'll actually experience comes down to how carefully the dose is titrated and how you eat, not which brand is on the pen. Rushing the dose to lose weight faster is the single most common self-inflicted cause of a miserable first month, on either drug. Smaller, lower-fat meals; eating slowly; stopping before you're full; staying hydrated; and giving each dose step a couple of weeks to settle — these blunt the side effects of Wegovy and Zepbound alike. The brand matters less than the ramp.

Managing side effects: the practical playbook (works for both)

Because the side-effect profiles are so similar, the management strategy is essentially the same for Wegovy and Zepbound — and getting it right is what separates people who stay on the drug and succeed from people who quit in a rough week two. Side effects are the number-one reason people stop a GLP-1, and the vast majority of that is preventable or manageable. Here's the playbook; our full side-effects guide goes deeper on each item.

Respect the titration — this is 80% of it. Nearly every bad first month traces to a dose raised too fast. The starter doses exist to let your stomach adapt; moving up before you've settled, or pushing to a higher dose to lose weight faster, is the single most common self-inflicted mistake on either drug. If a dose step brings side effects you can't comfortably manage, the fix is usually to hold at that dose longer, or step back down temporarily, not to power through — a conversation to have with your prescriber, who can slow the ramp without derailing your progress.

Eat for a stomach that's running slow. Almost every GI side effect comes from the drugs slowing your stomach, so the food strategy follows one rule: don't overload it. In practice that means smaller meals (your old portions are now often too much), lower-fat choices (fat slows emptying further and worsens nausea), eating slowly, and stopping before you feel full — because the gap between "satisfied" and "queasy" narrows on these drugs. Bland, simple foods sit better on rough days; greasy, heavy, or very large meals are the fastest route to nausea. None of this is deprivation; it's matching intake to a stomach that now holds and processes less at a time.

Stay ahead of constipation and hydration. Because you're eating and drinking less, constipation is common on both drugs. Fluids, fiber, movement, and sometimes an OTC stool softener or fiber supplement (cleared with your clinician) keep it from becoming a problem. Dehydration sneaks up when appetite is suppressed — you simply forget to drink — and it worsens both constipation and that generally-unwell feeling, so deliberate hydration is one of the highest-value habits on either drug.

Time the dose to your life. Some people find taking the weekly shot before a lighter-activity day, or in the evening, helps them ride out any transient nausea; others prefer mornings. There's no universal best day — the point is you can choose the timing that fits your week, and keep it consistent. And give each new dose a couple of weeks: the predictable pattern on both drugs is that side effects peak after a dose change and then ease as your body adapts, so an uncomfortable few days after stepping up is usually a phase, not a verdict.

Know the line between uncomfortable and dangerous. The playbook above handles ordinary GI discomfort, which is the overwhelming majority of what people experience. But certain symptoms mean stop-and-call rather than manage-and-continue: severe, persistent abdominal pain (especially radiating to the back), relentless vomiting or inability to keep fluids down, signs of a gallbladder attack, or any symptom that feels clearly out of the ordinary. These are uncommon, they don't meaningfully differ between the two drugs, and they're exactly why a real prescriber — not a questionnaire — belongs in this process. "Tough it out" is the right instinct for nausea and the wrong instinct for a red flag.

Muscle loss and body composition

One concern now comes up with both drugs more than almost any other, and it deserves a straight answer: are you losing muscle along with fat? The honest reply is yes, to a degree — and it's a shared trait of rapid weight loss generally, not a quirk of one brand over the other. Understanding it changes how you should eat and move while on either drug.

When anyone loses weight quickly — by any method, drug or not — a portion of the loss is lean mass (muscle and other non-fat tissue), not just fat. GLP-1 drugs produce large, relatively fast weight loss, so some lean-mass loss comes with the territory; studies of these drugs have measured a meaningful fraction of total loss as lean tissue. This isn't unique to Wegovy or Zepbound, and it isn't a reason to avoid them — but it is a reason to be deliberate, because preserving muscle protects your metabolism, your strength, and your long-term results. The concern is real; the panic some headlines attach to it is not.

The two levers that protect lean mass are well established and entirely in your control: adequate protein and resistance training. Eating enough protein (often harder on these drugs precisely because your appetite is suppressed, so it takes intention) gives your body the raw material to hold onto muscle, and strength training signals your body to keep it. People who lose weight on a GLP-1 while hitting their protein and lifting tend to lose a much healthier ratio of fat to muscle than people who just eat less of everything and skip the gym. This is the single most actionable thing in the body-composition conversation, and it applies identically to Wegovy and Zepbound.

Does the brand matter here? Not in a way that should drive your choice. Both drugs cause the same general pattern — large weight loss with some lean-mass component — and there's no strong evidence that one is meaningfully better than the other for preserving muscle at matched weight loss. Since Zepbound tends to produce more total weight loss, it may produce more absolute lean-mass loss simply because the overall loss is larger, but the proportion and the solution (protein + resistance training) are the same. Choosing between them on muscle grounds isn't well supported; choosing to train and eat protein whichever you take is.

There's also an encouraging side worth stating, because the muscle conversation can tip into fear. The weight loss these drugs produce improves many health markers that matter more than a body-composition scan — and for people with obesity, the metabolic and cardiovascular benefits of losing substantial weight are large and well documented (semaglutide's cardiovascular-outcome data is a case in point). The goal isn't to avoid all lean-mass change — that's unrealistic with meaningful weight loss — it's to minimize it with protein and strength work while you capture the broader benefits. Done that way, body composition is a thing you manage, not a reason to hesitate, on either drug.

Who shouldn't take Wegovy or Zepbound

These drugs are not for everyone, and the exclusions are essentially the same for both — another reason the choice between them is less about safety screening and more about fit. This is general information, not a substitute for a clinician's assessment, which is exactly the point: deciding whether a GLP-1 is safe for you is a medical judgment that requires a real history, which is why a legitimate program always includes a genuine prescriber.

Both Wegovy and Zepbound carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors (based on rodent studies), so neither is used in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or the genetic syndrome MEN 2. Both are generally avoided in people with a history of pancreatitis or certain serious gallbladder disease, used cautiously (or not at all) in pregnancy and breastfeeding — these drugs are not for weight loss during pregnancy — and require care in people with a history of severe gastrointestinal disease like gastroparesis. People taking insulin or sulfonylureas for diabetes need dose adjustments because of hypoglycemia risk. The authoritative drug-information pages at MedlinePlus (semaglutide, tirzepatide) list the warnings in full, and the NIH's NIDDK overview covers who these medications are and aren't for.

There's also an eligibility dimension separate from safety: these drugs are approved for specific populations — generally adults with obesity (a BMI at or above 30), or overweight (BMI at or above 27) with a weight-related condition like high blood pressure, high cholesterol, or type 2 diabetes. They are not approved or appropriate as a cosmetic shortcut for someone at a healthy weight, and a responsible prescriber will not hand them out on that basis. This matters for the online route specifically: a program that prescribes without establishing that you actually meet the medical criteria is a red flag, not a convenience.

The reason this belongs in a Wegovy-vs-Zepbound guide is to underline the single most important decision again: because the contraindications, warnings, and eligibility criteria are nearly identical between the two drugs, they don't help you pick between them — but they absolutely determine whether you should be on either, and that's a clinician's call. If a program lets you check a few boxes and buy a GLP-1 without any real evaluation of your history, the problem isn't which drug you chose; it's that no one with the training to catch a contraindication was ever in the loop. Choose a program where a licensed clinician actually evaluates you. That protects you more than any difference between the two molecules.

Dosing, titration, and how you take each one

Both drugs follow the same core logic: start at a low, barely-effective dose and step up over months to the dose that actually drives weight loss. This isn't bureaucratic caution — it's the entire tolerability strategy. The slow ramp lets your stomach adapt so that by the time you reach the stronger doses, the side effects are manageable. People who try to skip steps almost always pay in nausea, and often quit.

Wegovy (semaglutide) uses a roughly monthly step-up through a fixed ladder of doses — commonly described as 0.25 mg, then 0.5, 1.0, 1.7, and 2.4 mg once weekly — with each step held for about four weeks before moving up, reaching the 2.4 mg maintenance dose at around month five. The new 7.2 mg high dose sits above that, intended for people who've already tolerated 2.4 mg and want additional weight loss, at the cost of more GI side effects. Zepbound (tirzepatide) uses its own ladder — commonly 2.5 mg to start, then 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, and up to 15 mg — again stepping roughly monthly, with several possible maintenance doses depending on how much help you need and tolerate. The exact schedule your clinician uses may differ; both labels build in flexibility to slow down or hold a dose if side effects flare.

The practical upshot of the two ladders is similar: budget on the order of four to five months to reach a full maintenance dose, and don't judge the drug's effectiveness until you're there and have given it time. A common and costly misread is quitting at week six on a starter dose because "it's not working much" — of course it isn't; the starter dose is designed mainly to build tolerance, not to melt weight. The weight loss accelerates as you climb. This is identical in spirit for both brands.

On the how-you-take-it mechanics, they're alike: a once-weekly subcutaneous injection you do yourself, same day each week, rotating sites on the belly, thigh, or upper arm. Wegovy has historically used a single-use pre-filled pen; Zepbound comes as both single-dose pens and vials (the vials being part of how Lilly offers lower self-pay pricing). Both need refrigeration within their labeling, both use short fine needles, and both become a quick, unremarkable weekly habit for most people within a shot or two.

The one genuine fork in the "how you take it" question is semaglutide's oral option. The Wegovy pill (a 25 mg tablet, approved in late 2025 — Novo Nordisk, 2025) gives semaglutide a needle-free route, with its own particular rules (taken on an empty stomach with a small sip of water, then waiting before eating or drinking). Zepbound has no oral form. So if needle avoidance is a dealbreaker, the comparison tilts: a semaglutide pill exists, a tirzepatide pill (as Zepbound) does not. The oral pill's weight-loss results and the trade-offs against the injections are their own topic — see our weight-loss pill guide — but it's worth knowing the option is semaglutide-only when weighing the two brands.

Whichever you choose, the dosing takeaways are the same and worth repeating because they prevent the most common failures: respect the ramp, reach a real maintenance dose, give each step time, and never rush to a higher dose to speed up results. The schedule is the tolerability plan, not an obstacle to it.

What to expect, month by month

Whichever drug you choose, the arc of the first several months is broadly similar, and knowing it prevents the two classic mistakes: quitting too early because "it's not working," and judging the drug before you've reached an effective dose. Here's the realistic timeline for both Wegovy and Zepbound.

Month 1 (starter dose). You begin at a low dose designed mainly to build tolerance, not to drive weight loss. Expect modest appetite changes and, for many people, the first brush with side effects — some nausea, maybe constipation — especially in the first week or two. Weight loss this month is usually small, and that's by design; the starter dose is a ramp, not the destination. The work of month one is establishing the habit and letting your stomach adapt, not hitting a number.

Months 2–4 (stepping up). You climb the dose ladder roughly monthly. This is where appetite suppression becomes noticeable — smaller portions satisfy you, food noise quiets down — and where steady weight loss typically establishes itself. Each dose increase can bring a few days of renewed side effects that then settle, which is the normal pattern on both drugs. This is also the stretch where people who rushed the dose struggle most, and where patience and good food habits pay off. By the end of this window many people are approaching or at a maintenance dose.

Months 4–6 (reaching maintenance). You arrive at a full maintenance dose, and this is where the drug is doing what it was designed to do. Weight loss continues at a meaningful pace for most people, and the side effects that were sharpest during titration have usually eased as your body adapted. This is the earliest point at which it's fair to judge how well the drug is working for you — judging at month two, on a starter dose, is like rating a car by how it idles. If results are disappointing at a tolerated maintenance dose after a fair trial, that's the moment to discuss options with your prescriber, including a switch.

Months 6–12 and beyond. Weight loss typically continues but gradually slows as you approach a new set point; the big trials ran to around 68–72 weeks for a reason — that's roughly how long the major loss unfolds. Many people reach a plateau in the second half of the first year, which is normal and not a failure. The central question shifts from "how fast am I losing" to "how do I sustain this" — because, as covered above, stopping tends to bring weight back. This is where the chronic-treatment framing becomes concrete: the plan is ongoing, and the drug you can afford and tolerate long-term beats the one with the better trial average that you quit. Our guide on realistic results by the numbers and the first-four-weeks guide go deeper on the early and long arcs.

The single most useful thing to internalize from this timeline: effectiveness reveals itself at maintenance dose, months into treatment — not in the first few weeks. Both drugs are slow-burn by design. Expecting dramatic early loss sets you up to quit a drug that was about to work; expecting the real results at months four through twelve sets you up to actually get them. That patience applies equally to Wegovy and Zepbound, and it's worth more than picking the "right" one.

The compounded alternative — and its trade-offs

You can't honestly compare Wegovy and Zepbound in 2026 without addressing the elephant in the room: compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide, sold by many online programs for a fraction of the brand price. For a lot of people, the real decision isn't Wegovy vs Zepbound at all — it's branded vs compounded. Here's the straight version, because this is where misinformation runs thickest in both directions.

Compounded GLP-1s use the same active molecules — semaglutide or tirzepatide — but are mixed by a compounding pharmacy rather than manufactured and FDA-approved as a finished product. That's the crucial distinction: compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, which means they haven't gone through the agency's review for safety, effectiveness, and manufacturing quality the way Wegovy and Zepbound have. The FDA has been explicit about its concerns with unapproved GLP-1 drugs used for weight loss, including dosing errors and quality variability. Compounding is legal and long-established for legitimate needs, and many licensed pharmacies do it carefully — but the quality floor is the pharmacy's, not the FDA's, and that's the trade you're making.

Why do people choose it anyway? Cost, mostly. Where branded cash pricing runs a few hundred dollars a month, compounded programs often run from around $49 to $199 a month, medication included. For people without coverage, that gap is the difference between starting treatment and not. There's also a legitimacy question that shifted recently: compounded GLP-1s surged during the brand shortages, and the rules around them changed as those shortages resolved — a regulatory story we track in detail in is compounded semaglutide still legal. The short version is that the landscape is narrower and more scrutinized than it was at the peak, which makes who you buy from matter more than ever.

So how should you weigh it? Honestly, like this. Branded (Wegovy/Zepbound) buys you FDA approval, manufacturer quality control, a known dose in a known device, and the exact products the trials studied — for more money. Compounded buys you a much lower price and the same core molecule — at the cost of FDA approval and with real variability in pharmacy quality, so vetting the provider is on you. Neither choice is "the scam" and neither is "obviously right." If you can get branded covered by insurance for ~$25/month, that's usually the clear winner. If you're paying cash and brand pricing is out of reach, a carefully vetted compounded program from a licensed clinic is how a lot of people access treatment they otherwise couldn't — with eyes open about the trade-off. One firm line regardless of which you choose: vet the pharmacy and the clinician. The compounded market's quality varies more than the branded market's, so the red flags matter more — no real medical intake, prices too good to be true, no licensed prescriber, vague sourcing, pressure to prepay for many months. The molecule being identical doesn't make every compounded product equivalent; the preparation and the pharmacy are where the risk lives. Choose a program that takes safety seriously, and the compounded route becomes a reasonable, lower-cost path; choose carelessly, and you've taken on the one risk these drugs don't otherwise carry.

Switching between them (and starting over)

Because the two drugs are so often framed as rivals, people assume picking one is a permanent commitment. It isn't. Switching between semaglutide and tirzepatide is common, legitimate, and sometimes the smartest move — and knowing that takes a lot of pressure off the initial choice.

There are two main reasons people switch. The first is tolerability: someone gets side effects on one drug that they can't manage even with a careful titration, switches to the other, and does fine. Because the drugs aren't identical — different molecules, tirzepatide's extra GIP action — individual guts respond to them differently in ways no one can predict in advance. A rough ride on one is not a verdict on the whole drug class; it's often just a reason to try the other. The second reason is plateau or insufficient results: someone on semaglutide who's stalled below their goal at a tolerated dose may switch to tirzepatide for its higher ceiling, or vice versa for other reasons. Either switch is a normal clinical decision, made with a prescriber, not a sign anything failed.

The mechanics matter, though, and this is firmly a clinician's call, not a DIY move. Switching isn't a simple swap at equivalent doses — the two drugs don't map onto each other one-to-one, so your prescriber will choose an appropriate starting dose on the new drug and usually re-titrate up, which means a short step back down in dose intensity while your body re-adapts. Done right, it's smooth; done wrong (jumping to a high dose on the new drug to "keep up"), it reproduces the rough first month. The same "start low, go slow" logic that governs starting governs switching.

There's also a cost-and-access angle to switching that's easy to miss. If one drug's price, coverage, or supply changes — a promotion ends, a formulary shifts, a program raises prices — switching to the other (or from branded to compounded, or compounded to branded) can be a reasonable response. The point is that you retain optionality. You are not locked in by your first choice, which is exactly why the first choice shouldn't feel high-stakes. Start with whichever makes sense on today's facts (coverage, budget, priorities), give it a real trial, and switch if the evidence of your own experience says to.

One caution: switching repeatedly or impulsively — chasing faster results by hopping drugs every few weeks — is counterproductive, because you never give either drug the stable titration it needs to show what it can do. Switching is a tool for a specific problem (intolerable side effects, a genuine plateau, a cost or access change), used deliberately with a clinician. Used that way, it's one of the quiet advantages of there being two good options instead of one.

Common myths about Wegovy vs Zepbound

This comparison attracts more confident misinformation than almost any other in the weight-loss space. Here are the claims worth correcting, because believing them leads to bad decisions.

Myth: "They're basically the same drug." They're the same class and share a lot of biology, but they're different molecules with a measurable difference in results — Zepbound lost meaningfully more weight in the head-to-head trial. "Same class" is not "same outcome." On the other hand, they're similar enough that for many people either will work well, so the opposite myth — that one is a miracle and the other is useless — is equally wrong.

Myth: "More side effects means it's working better." False, and costly. Side-effect intensity is not a weight-loss gauge; plenty of people with minimal nausea lose plenty of weight, and pushing the dose to feel more side effects doesn't buy more results — it just buys more misery and more quitting. Tellingly, in the head-to-head trial the more effective drug (Zepbound) had fewer people quit over side effects. Effectiveness and side effects are related but not the same lever.

Myth: "The new high-dose Wegovy definitely beats Zepbound now." Overclaiming. The 7.2 mg Wegovy dose does push semaglutide's results higher and narrows the gap — but it was never tested head-to-head against Zepbound, so claiming it ties or wins goes beyond the evidence. The honest statement is that it makes Wegovy a stronger contender than the 13.7% head-to-head figure suggests, not that it has overtaken tirzepatide.

Myth: "Compounded is the same as the brand, just cheaper." Same molecule, different regulatory reality. Compounded versions aren't FDA-approved and their quality depends on the pharmacy, not the agency — which can be fine with a careful, licensed provider and risky with a careless one. "Same active ingredient" is true; "therefore identical and equally safe" doesn't follow.

Myth: "You take it for a few months, lose the weight, and you're done." The hardest myth to let go of. These treat a chronic condition; stopping tends to bring weight back, as the withdrawal data shows. Whichever drug you choose, the realistic plan is long-term, which is exactly why cost, tolerability, and sustainability matter as much as the trial average. Our guide on stopping a GLP-1 covers the regain data honestly.

Myth: "The one with the bigger trial number is the one I'll lose the most on." Trial numbers are averages with huge individual spread. Your result depends on biology, dose, titration, and adherence far more than on the brand's headline percentage. Tilt the odds with the evidence, yes — but expect a personal range, not the brochure number.

The GLP-1 field is moving faster than almost any area of medicine, and a few developments on the near horizon are worth knowing about before you commit — not because you should wait (the drugs available now work), but because they affect how you think about flexibility and cost.

The pills are arriving. For years these drugs meant injections; that's changing. Semaglutide now has an oral form — the Wegovy pill (a 25 mg tablet) — supported by its own trial program (the OASIS studies; see OASIS 4, NEJM 2025). And a genuinely new kind of pill has arrived from the tirzepatide maker's side of the field: orforglipron, an oral small-molecule GLP-1, approved as Foundayo (Eli Lilly, 2026) with its own trial support (the ATTAIN program; ATTAIN-1, NEJM 2025). These matter to the Wegovy-vs-Zepbound decision because they expand the needle-free options and will, over time, reshape pricing as more products compete. Our weight-loss pill guide covers how the pills stack up against the injections.

Higher and more flexible dosing. The 2026 approval of the 7.2 mg high-dose Wegovy is part of a broader trend toward giving each drug a wider dose range, which means more room to push for additional weight loss (at the cost of more side effects) and more room to find a tolerable maintenance level. Expect the "ceiling" on both molecules to keep evolving, which is one more reason today's head-to-head average is a snapshot, not a permanent verdict.

Even stronger agents are in trials. Beyond semaglutide and tirzepatide, next-generation molecules targeting additional hormone pathways have posted striking weight-loss numbers in earlier-stage research. They aren't approved for general use yet and shouldn't factor into a decision you're making now, but they signal the direction: more options, higher ceilings, and — eventually — more price competition. The practical implication is don't over-agonize over a permanent choice. You're not marrying a molecule. The field will offer more routes over the next few years, switching is normal, and the right move is to start effective treatment now with whichever drug fits your coverage, budget, and medical picture — not to wait for a perfect future option while carrying the health costs of obesity in the meantime.

One caution about the pipeline hype: emerging trial numbers are reported under ideal conditions and often shrink in real-world use, and "approved" is a very different thing from "in a late-stage trial." Treat anything that isn't FDA-approved and available as interesting context, not a reason to delay. The two drugs this guide compares are proven, available, and effective today; the pipeline is a reason for optimism about the future, not a reason to postpone the present.

Wegovy vs Zepbound: frequently asked questions

Is Zepbound better than Wegovy for weight loss?

On average, yes — in the only head-to-head trial (SURMOUNT-5), Zepbound (tirzepatide) produced more weight loss than Wegovy (semaglutide): about 20.2% versus 13.7% of body weight over 72 weeks, and with fewer people stopping over side effects. But "better on average" isn't "better for everyone." Wegovy has standout cardiovascular-outcome data, an oral pill option, a longer track record, and a new higher 7.2 mg dose that narrows the weight-loss gap. For many people the deciding factor ends up being insurance coverage, cost, side-effect tolerance, or a clinician's read of their medical history — not the trial average.

How much weight will I lose on each?

Expect a range, not a guarantee. In the trials, Wegovy averaged around 14.9% of body weight (STEP 1) and Zepbound around 21% at the top dose (SURMOUNT-1), with the head-to-head showing 13.7% vs 20.2%. But individual results spanned from almost nothing to 30%+, driven by biology, dose, titration, and how long you stay on. The averages tilt toward Zepbound; your personal result depends on far more than the brand.

Which has fewer side effects?

The profiles are broadly similar — both are mostly gastrointestinal (nausea, constipation, diarrhea), worst at the start and each dose increase, then fading. In the head-to-head trial, fewer people actually stopped Zepbound over side effects (about 2.7% vs 5.6%), which cuts against the idea that the stronger drug is rougher. But tolerance is individual: some people do better on one than the other, and careful titration matters more than the brand.

Can I switch from Wegovy to Zepbound or vice versa?

Yes, and it's common. People switch for side effects they can't manage or for a plateau, and because individual response varies unpredictably, someone who struggles on one often does well on the other. It's a clinician's decision, not a DIY swap — the drugs don't map one-to-one, so your prescriber will set an appropriate starting dose on the new drug and re-titrate up.

Is compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide as good as Wegovy or Zepbound?

It's the same active molecule but not the same product: compounded versions aren't FDA-approved, so quality depends on the compounding pharmacy rather than the agency's review. Many licensed programs do it carefully and at a fraction of the brand price, which is why a lot of cash-paying people choose it. The trade-off is real — you give up FDA approval and manufacturer quality control for the lower cost — so vetting the provider matters more than with the brand. If you can get the brand covered by insurance for around $25/month, that's usually the better deal.

What does each cost in 2026?

With commercial insurance that covers the drug, a manufacturer savings card can bring either to roughly $25/month. Paying cash for the brand directly, Wegovy runs around $349/month (with lower intro pricing on early fills) and Zepbound roughly $299–$499/month by dose. Compounded alternatives from online programs often run about $49–$199/month but aren't FDA-approved. All of these change often — verify the current number for your dose before deciding.

Can I get Wegovy or Zepbound online?

Yes. Legitimate telehealth programs can do a proper medical intake, connect you with a licensed clinician, prescribe the branded drug, and help with insurance and savings cards; others sell lower-cost compounded alternatives. The non-negotiables are a real medical intake (not just a payment form), a licensed prescriber, transparent ongoing pricing, and clear refill and cancellation terms. See our provider reviews and the guide on spotting a legit online clinic.

Does the new higher-dose Wegovy (7.2 mg) beat Zepbound?

Not proven. The 7.2 mg dose, approved in 2026, pushes semaglutide's average weight loss up toward the high-teens-to-~20% range, narrowing the gap with Zepbound — but it was never tested head-to-head against tirzepatide, so no one can honestly claim it ties or wins. It makes Wegovy a stronger contender than the standard-dose head-to-head figure suggests, at the cost of more side effects at the top dose.

Quick glossary

Semaglutide — the molecule in Wegovy (weight loss) and Ozempic (diabetes); a single-hormone GLP-1 receptor agonist. Tirzepatide — the molecule in Zepbound (weight loss) and Mounjaro (diabetes); a dual GLP-1/GIP agonist. GLP-1 / GIP — gut hormones the drugs mimic to reduce appetite and slow stomach emptying; tirzepatide hits both, semaglutide hits one. Titration — the gradual dose step-up over months that keeps side effects tolerable. Compounded — a drug mixed by a pharmacy rather than manufactured and FDA-approved as a finished product; same molecule, different regulatory status. Maintenance dose — the ongoing effective dose you reach after titrating up. Head-to-head trial — a study giving the same population one drug or the other and comparing directly (here, SURMOUNT-5).

The honest verdict

Put it all together and the picture is clear without being simplistic. Zepbound wins the weight-loss contest — it lost more in the only trial that compared them directly, and more in its own pivotal trial, with fewer people quitting over side effects. If maximum likely weight loss is your single priority and nothing else overrides it, that's the tilt. Wegovy is the proven all-rounder — the longer track record, the landmark heart-outcome data, an oral pill option, and a new higher dose that narrows the weight-loss gap — and for people with cardiovascular risk, a needle aversion, or a formulary that prefers it, it's often the better personal choice despite the lower head-to-head number.

But the honest meta-verdict is that for most people, the decision is made by cost, coverage, and sustainability more than by the trial average. The best drug is the one you can get covered or afford, tolerate well enough to stay on, and keep taking long enough to matter — because stopping brings the weight back whichever brand you chose. Take whichever your insurance covers; if you're paying cash, choose by your priority and consider a vetted compounded alternative if brand pricing is out of reach; give it a real titration and several months; and do all of it with a genuine prescriber rather than a checkout questionnaire. Get those fundamentals right and either of these drugs can change your health. Get them wrong — picking by hype, rushing the dose, or buying from a program that skips the medicine — and the best molecule in the world won't save a bad plan.
