The short version

GLP-1 medications — semaglutide (Wegovy, Ozempic) and tirzepatide (Zepbound, Mounjaro) — are the most effective weight-loss drugs ever brought to market, and most people now start them through an online telehealth program rather than an in-person clinic. In the pivotal trials, semaglutide produced about 15% average weight loss and tirzepatide up to roughly 21%. What are GLP-1 medications, and how do they actually work?

GLP-1 stands for glucagon-like peptide-1, a hormone your gut releases naturally after you eat. It does several things at once: it tells your pancreas to release insulin, it slows how fast food leaves your stomach, and it signals your brain that you are full. The weight-loss medications in this category are GLP-1 receptor agonists — molecules engineered to mimic that hormone but last far longer in the body, so a single weekly injection keeps the "I'm satisfied" signal switched on for seven days instead of a few minutes. Harvard Health's explainer is a good plain-English primer on the biology if you want to go deeper.

In practice, people describe three changes. First, the constant background hum of hunger quiets down — the "food noise" that pulls your attention to the kitchen all afternoon. Second, meals feel satisfying sooner, so portions shrink without a sense of deprivation. Third, the reward you used to get from a second helping or a late-night snack flattens out. None of this is willpower; it is pharmacology acting on the same appetite-regulation circuits that make dieting so hard in the first place. That is why GLP-1s work where most diets fail: they change the biology of appetite rather than asking you to out-muscle it.

It helps to understand that obesity is now formally treated as a chronic disease, not a lifestyle failing. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases describes prescription weight-management medicines as tools used alongside diet and activity for people whose health is affected by excess weight — not shortcuts, and not standalone cures. The medication lowers the biological resistance; the habits you build around it decide how much of the result sticks.

Semaglutide vs. tirzepatide: one receptor or two

There are two leading molecules, and the difference between them is mechanical. Semaglutide acts on the single GLP-1 receptor. Tirzepatide is a dual agonist: it hits the GLP-1 receptor and a second gut-hormone receptor called GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide). Engaging two appetite-and-metabolism pathways instead of one is the leading explanation for why tirzepatide tends to produce more weight loss on average in head-to-head trial data. We dig into that comparison in depth below and in our dedicated tirzepatide vs. semaglutide guide.

Both are given as a once-weekly subcutaneous injection using a small pen or syringe — the needle is short and fine, similar to what people with diabetes have used for decades. Both are started at a deliberately low dose and increased slowly over weeks or months, a process called titration that exists to limit side effects. Neither is a "take it and forget it" drug: the dose, the timing and the way your program handles side effects all matter, which is a recurring theme in this guide.

Why these drugs became a weight-loss story

Semaglutide and tirzepatide were first developed and approved for type 2 diabetes — Ozempic and Mounjaro are the diabetes brands. Clinicians and trial participants noticed substantial weight loss as a "side effect," and the manufacturers ran dedicated obesity trials at higher doses. Those trials led to the weight-management brands: Wegovy (semaglutide) and Zepbound (tirzepatide). So when you hear "Ozempic for weight loss," what people usually mean is semaglutide — the same molecule, studied and dosed specifically for weight management under a different brand name. Understanding that the molecule and the brand are different things will save you a lot of confusion when you compare programs, because some advertise the molecule (semaglutide) and some advertise a brand (Wegovy).

Which GLP-1 medications are used for weight loss in 2026?

Here is the practical landscape, because the names multiply quickly and the marketing blurs them on purpose.

  • Semaglutide — the molecule. Approved for weight management as Wegovy and for type 2 diabetes as Ozempic. There is also an oral semaglutide tablet (Rybelsus, diabetes). The FDA approved Wegovy for chronic weight management in 2021. Drug-level patient information is on MedlinePlus.
  • Tirzepatide — the dual GIP/GLP-1 molecule. Approved for weight management as Zepbound and for type 2 diabetes as Mounjaro. The FDA approved Zepbound in late 2023; patient information is on MedlinePlus.
  • Compounded semaglutide / tirzepatide — the same molecules prepared by a compounding pharmacy rather than the brand manufacturer. Cheaper, widely sold through telehealth, and not FDA-approved. We devote a full section to what that means for cost, legality and safety.
  • Oral and "alternative" formats — some programs offer sublingual drops, troches or tablets of compounded GLP-1. These are convenient but less studied than the injectable versions the trials were built on; treat bold claims about them with extra caution.

Two older injectable GLP-1s — liraglutide (Saxenda) and exenatide — still exist but are largely eclipsed for weight loss by the once-weekly pair above, because they require daily dosing and produce less weight loss. You will occasionally see newer molecules such as retatrutide in the news; as of this writing they are investigational and not something you can legitimately buy from a telehealth program, so be deeply skeptical of anyone selling them.

A word on "research peptides"

If a website sells semaglutide or tirzepatide labeled "for research use only" or "not for human consumption," do not buy it, full stop. That labeling is a legal dodge that puts an unregulated, unverified product into your body with no clinician, no real pharmacy and no recourse. The FDA has warned specifically about unapproved and illegally marketed GLP-1 products. A legitimate program always involves a licensed clinician and a named pharmacy.

How much weight can you actually lose on a GLP-1?

This is the question everyone wants answered honestly, so here is the honest version: a lot, on average, but with a wide spread, and only while the biology and the habits both stay in place. The headline numbers come from large, randomized, placebo-controlled trials published in the New England Journal of Medicine.

In the STEP 1 trial of semaglutide 2.4 mg, adults with overweight or obesity lost about 14.9% of their body weight on average over 68 weeks, versus roughly 2.4% on placebo; around 86% of participants lost at least 5% of their weight, and about a third lost 20% or more (Wilding et al., NEJM 2021). For a 220-pound person, 15% is roughly 33 pounds.

In the SURMOUNT-1 trial of tirzepatide, the average weight reduction climbed with the dose: about 15% at 5 mg, 19.5% at 10 mg and 20.9% at 15 mg over 72 weeks, versus roughly 3% on placebo, with more than nine in ten participants on the top dose losing at least 5% (Jastreboff et al., NEJM 2022). That is the single clearest reason tirzepatide has a reputation for stronger results — the trial averages are simply higher.

Three caveats keep these numbers honest. First, these are averages: some people lose far more, a meaningful minority lose little, and a small group cannot tolerate the drug at all. Second, the trial results reflect a full titration to the top dose plus structured lifestyle support over more than a year — not a month at a starter dose. Third, the weight loss continues only while the medication and habits continue; we cover what happens when you stop in its own section, because it is the part the marketing most wants you to forget.

Beyond the scale, the trials also measured health markers that matter more than a number: blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol and waist circumference generally improved. And in the SELECT trial, semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced the risk of major cardiovascular events by about 20% in people with established heart disease and overweight or obesity but without diabetes — the first hard evidence that this class does more than move weight. That is clinically significant, and it is one reason a real clinician evaluation (rather than an auto-approved form) is worth paying for: the right candidate gets more than cosmetic benefit.

How fast does it come off?

Slowly at first, by design. Because you start at a low dose and step up over weeks, early weight loss is usually modest — a few pounds in the first month is normal and nothing to panic about. Most people see the rate pick up as they reach effective doses around months two to four, then taper toward a plateau over the following months as the body finds a new set point. Expecting dramatic month-one results is the fastest route to quitting a drug that was actually working; patience through titration is part of the treatment.

Semaglutide vs. tirzepatide: which should you choose?

On the trial averages, tirzepatide produces more weight loss. If that were the only variable, the choice would be trivial. It is not, and a good program will help you weigh several factors rather than simply pushing the one with the bigger headline.

  • Efficacy. Advantage tirzepatide on average, especially at higher doses. But semaglutide's ~15% is still transformative for most people, and plenty do better on it than the averages suggest.
  • Tolerability. Both cause gastrointestinal side effects, mainly early in titration. Some people tolerate one noticeably better than the other; there is no way to know in advance which, which is why the ability to switch matters.
  • Cardiovascular evidence. Semaglutide has the SELECT outcome data behind it for people with existing heart disease. Tirzepatide's long-term outcome trials are maturing. If cardiovascular risk is part of your picture, raise it with your clinician.
  • Cost and availability. Pricing moves month to month and differs between branded and compounded forms. Sometimes one is markedly cheaper or more reliably in stock than the other through a given program.
  • Format preference. Both are weekly injections in their standard forms. If needles are a dealbreaker, that is worth discussing, though the once-weekly injectable is what the strongest evidence supports.

Our practical guidance: do not fixate on the molecule before you pick the program. The program's flexibility — whether it can switch you between semaglutide and tirzepatide, and between compounded and branded, without making you start over — often matters more than which one you begin with, because your tolerance and your budget are things you discover by living with the drug, not by reading about it. The programs that handle this well quietly save people from quitting. The honest default

If you want the strongest average results and have no specific reason to prefer semaglutide, tirzepatide is the statistically stronger starting point. If cost, cardiovascular evidence, or a clinician's read of your history points to semaglutide, that is a perfectly good choice that still produces excellent results for most people. There is no universally "best" molecule — only the best fit for your body, budget and history.

Branded, compounded, or pills: what's actually FDA-approved?

This is the most consequential thing to understand before you buy, because it drives both the price and the risk profile, and because the marketing works hard to blur it.

Branded semaglutide (Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Zepbound) are FDA-approved. That means the exact product was tested in the trials above, is made under the manufacturer's quality control, and carries an FDA-reviewed label. You pay for that certainty: branded weight-loss GLP-1s are expensive and, for weight loss specifically, are often not covered by insurance.

Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide are prepared by compounding pharmacies that mix the active ingredient into an injectable. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved — the agency does not verify their safety, effectiveness or quality before they reach you. Compounding is legal and long-established for specific situations, but it is a different regulatory category, and the FDA's own Q&A on compounding is worth reading once so you understand what you are and are not getting.

503A vs. 503B: the pharmacy distinction that matters

Not all compounding pharmacies are the same, and the two categories are defined in federal law. A 503A pharmacy compounds a medication for an individual patient against a specific prescription — think traditional pharmacy compounding. A 503B outsourcing facility registers with the FDA, is inspected to tighter standards closer to a manufacturer's, and can make larger batches. Neither produces an FDA-approved drug, but a 503B generally operates under more oversight. A program that names its pharmacy and its type is being transparent; one that will not tell you where your medication is made is waving a flag. The FDA explains the categories in its human drug compounding materials.

Why compounded GLP-1 exists at all — and why the ground keeps shifting

For a stretch of 2022–2024, demand for semaglutide and tirzepatide outran supply, and both appeared on the FDA's drug shortage list. Federal law lets compounders prepare copies of a drug that is in shortage, which is how a large compounded-GLP-1 market grew up almost overnight. When the shortages were declared resolved, that legal basis narrowed, and the market entered a gray, shifting zone with transition periods and enforcement discretion that continue to evolve. The practical implication for you: compounded access and its legal footing can change, sometimes quickly, so a provider's flexibility to move you to a branded product if compounded supply tightens is a real feature, not a nicety.

The real risks of compounded products

Cheaper is not free of trade-offs. The documented concerns are specific, not hand-wavy:

  • Dosing errors. The FDA has warned about dosing errors with compounded semaglutide, including people drawing up the wrong amount from vials and syringes. A program that supplies pre-filled pens or crystal-clear instructions reduces this risk.
  • Salt forms. Some compounders have used semaglutide salt forms (such as semaglutide sodium or acetate) that differ from the base used in the approved products; the FDA has flagged products using these forms.
  • Variable quality. Because the product is not FDA-approved, consistency depends entirely on the pharmacy. A reputable 503B with third-party testing is a different proposition from an anonymous operation.

None of this means compounded is categorically unsafe — millions of doses are used, and a transparent program working with a reputable pharmacy is a reasonable, far cheaper path for many people. It means you should know what you are choosing and insist on transparency. We go deeper in compounded vs. brand vs. pills.

What about GLP-1 pills?

Oral options exist — an approved oral semaglutide for diabetes, newer oral agents in development, and compounded sublingual or troche formats sold by some telehealth programs. Pills are appealing if you dislike injections, but two cautions apply: the strongest weight-loss evidence is for the injectables, and compounded oral formats are less studied than the injectable versions the trials validated. If needle-avoidance is driving your choice, have that conversation with a clinician rather than assuming an oral compounded product performs like the injectable in the trials.

What does a GLP-1 really cost online?

This is where most people get hurt, so we are going to be blunt and specific. There are three numbers that matter, and the programs are very good at showing you only the friendliest one.

  1. The intro price — the "$39 first month" or "$79 to start" teaser. Real, but temporary. It exists to get your card on file.
  2. The maintenance price — what you actually pay each month once you reach the dose you stay on, often two to five times the intro. This is the number that decides affordability.
  3. The year-one cost — the maintenance price times twelve, plus any prepaid bundle. This is the number that decides value.

Across the programs we track, typical real maintenance cost runs around $138 a month for compounded GLP-1 (most people land between about $109 and $169), while branded Wegovy or Zepbound without insurance typically runs around $335 a month and can be higher. Membership fees, prepay bundles and the overcharge trap

Two pricing structures create most of the "I got overcharged" complaints. The first is the membership model, where a monthly fee sits on top of the medication cost. A "$39 membership" plus the drug can quietly become $250–$300 all-in once you add the medication and titrate up. The second is the prepaid bundle, which unlocks the lowest headline price by charging for several months up front. Bundles genuinely lower the monthly rate — but only if the provider ships reliably for all those months, and only if you tolerate the drug. If a refill is late or the medication does not suit you, "no refund" and "can't cancel" turn a good deal into a trap. Every honest comparison should quote the price you keep paying, not the one that gets you in the door. A program that is cheap in month one and expensive at your maintenance dose can cost more over a year than a program that was transparent up front. How do online GLP-1 programs actually work?

The mechanics are more standardized than the marketing suggests. Nearly every legitimate program follows the same five steps; the differences are in the quality of each one.

  1. Medical intake. You complete a health questionnaire — weight and height (for BMI), medical history, current medications, allergies and relevant family history. Honesty here is not optional; it is how a clinician screens out the contraindications we cover below.
  2. Clinician review. A licensed prescriber reviews your intake. In the better programs this is a genuine evaluation — sometimes a video visit, sometimes a structured asynchronous review with follow-up questions — and the clinician can decline to prescribe if a GLP-1 is not appropriate for you. In the weakest programs it is a rubber stamp. This step is where most of the real medical value, and most of the quality difference, lives.
  3. Prescription and pharmacy. If appropriate, the clinician sends a prescription to a pharmacy — a compounding pharmacy for compounded products, a retail or specialty pharmacy for branded ones. A transparent program names the pharmacy.
  4. Delivery. The medication ships to your door, usually cold-chain for the injectables, with supplies and instructions. Expect a day or several depending on the provider.
  5. Ongoing care. The part that separates a program you stick with from one you quietly cancel: dose adjustments as you titrate, a way to reach someone when side effects hit, refills, and — in the best programs — coaching and check-ins that continue past month one.

Whether you even need a prescription is a common question, and the answer is yes, always: GLP-1s are prescription medications, and any service offering them without a clinician evaluation is not one to trust. We cover the specifics in do you need a prescription for weight-loss meds online.

Telehealth vs. your own doctor

The medicine and the need for a prescription are identical whether you go through a telehealth program or your primary-care physician. The difference is convenience and structure versus depth of oversight. Online programs bundle the visit, prescription, pharmacy and follow-up into one smooth flow, often with coaching built in, and they are frequently cheaper for compounded products than anything you can arrange locally. A physician who knows your full history offers deeper continuity and may navigate insurance for branded drugs more effectively. If you have a complex medical history, the in-person route — or a telehealth program with serious clinical oversight — is the safer call. For a straightforward candidate who wants to start efficiently, a reputable online program is a legitimate, mainstream choice.

How to choose a safe, legitimate program (and spot a bad one)

The industry grew fast, and the FDA sent warning letters to dozens of telehealth and compounding operations as it did. Most programs are legitimate; a meaningful minority cut corners. Run any program past these five checks before you hand over a card — this is the same framework we use when we score them, and it is expanded in how to spot a legit online clinic.

  1. Licensed in your state. A clinician must be licensed where you live to prescribe to you. Confirm the service actually operates in your state rather than assuming nationwide coverage.
  2. Named pharmacy and type. The program should tell you which pharmacy makes your medication and whether it is a 503A or 503B facility. "We don't disclose that" is a red flag, not a trade secret.
  3. A real clinician signs off. There should be a genuine review by a licensed prescriber — ideally named — not an instant auto-approval from a form with no human in the loop.
  4. You can cancel online. If you cannot find how to cancel before you pay, assume it is hard on purpose. Reputable programs make leaving as easy as joining.
  5. Refunds in writing. Especially for prepaid bundles, know the refund terms if a refill is delayed or the drug does not suit you. Get it in writing before, not after.

Red flags that should end the conversation

Walk away from any service that: guarantees a prescription before any medical review; sells "research-only" or "not for human use" peptides; will not name its pharmacy; has no way to reach a human about side effects; pressures you into a large prepaid plan with no clear refund policy; or makes specific weight-loss promises ("lose 30 pounds guaranteed"). Legitimate medicine does not work on guarantees, and legitimate programs do not hide the basics.

Signals of a genuinely good program

The inverse is just as useful. The programs worth paying for tend to share a profile: a clinician you can actually message between visits; a clear, itemized price with the real maintenance cost stated up front; flexibility to switch molecules or move between compounded and branded without starting over; a named, reputable pharmacy; coaching or check-ins that continue past onboarding; and a transparent, no-drama cancellation path. None of those are flashy. All of them correlate with people staying on treatment long enough to get results — which, in the end, is the only outcome that matters.

What to expect: the first weeks, dosing and titration

Starting a GLP-1 is a gradual on-ramp, not a switch you flip. Knowing the shape of it in advance prevents the two most common early mistakes: panicking at modest week-one results, and quitting over side effects that were always going to fade.

You begin at a low starter dose — deliberately below the dose that drives most of the weight loss — and increase in steps every few weeks, a schedule set by the product and your clinician. This titration exists for one reason: to let your gut adjust and keep side effects manageable. Rushing it is the main cause of miserable early weeks. A good program paces your increases to your tolerance rather than a rigid calendar, and slows down if you are struggling.

In the first four weeks, expect appetite changes to show up before dramatic scale changes. Many people notice the "food noise" quieting within days to a couple of weeks; the weight follows more slowly. A few pounds in the first month is a normal, healthy pace — not a disappointment. We walk through this window day by day in new to GLP-1s: the first 4 weeks.

Practical habits that make titration easier

  • Eat smaller, slower meals. Because the drug slows gastric emptying, large or fast meals are the fastest route to nausea. Stop at comfortably satisfied, not full.
  • Favor protein and fluids. Adequate protein protects muscle while you lose weight; staying hydrated eases constipation, a common complaint.
  • Ease off trigger foods early. Very fatty, fried or heavy meals tend to sit badly in the first weeks. Many people find their tolerance for them drops anyway.
  • Keep moving. Light activity helps digestion, mood and muscle retention. You do not need a punishing program; consistency beats intensity.
  • Tell your program early if you are struggling. A dose hold or slower step-up is often the fix. Suffering in silence is how people quit a drug that would have worked.

Side effects, and how to manage them

The side effects of GLP-1s are real, usually manageable, and concentrated in the early weeks of titration and after each dose increase. Being prepared is most of the battle.

The common ones are gastrointestinal: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation and reflux. In the STEP 1 trial, nausea was reported by roughly 44% of participants at some point, and most GI effects were mild to moderate and faded with time (Wilding et al., 2021). They are most intense right after a dose step-up and tend to settle as your body adapts. Smaller meals, hydration and a patient titration are the first-line fixes; a clinician can advise on dose timing and, where appropriate, symptom relief. For official, drug-specific side-effect information, see the MedlinePlus pages for semaglutide and tirzepatide.

Less common but important issues include gallbladder problems (rapid weight loss itself raises gallstone risk), pancreatitis (uncommon, but severe, persistent abdominal pain warrants urgent care), dehydration from GI losses, and low blood sugar, particularly if you also take insulin or sulfonylureas for diabetes. There is also ongoing attention to muscle-mass loss during rapid weight loss, which is why protein intake and resistance activity matter. None of these should scare you off a medication that transforms health for many people — they are reasons to be under real clinical care rather than buying unmonitored product from an anonymous website.

When to seek urgent care

Severe, persistent abdominal pain (especially radiating to the back), signs of an allergic reaction, persistent vomiting with dehydration, or symptoms of a gallbladder attack are not "ride it out" situations — seek medical care. This is information, not medical advice; your prescriber's guidance always comes first.

Safety, contraindications and who should not take a GLP-1

GLP-1s are not for everyone, and the screening a real clinician does is the reason the intake step is not a formality. Some conditions are firm contraindications or require careful judgment.

  • Personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or MEN 2. These carry a boxed warning for this drug class and are generally a contraindication. A rodent-study signal for thyroid C-cell tumors drives the caution.
  • History of pancreatitis. Requires careful consideration and clinician judgment.
  • Pregnancy or trying to conceive. GLP-1s are not used in pregnancy; plans to conceive change the calculus, and the drug is typically stopped well before. If you could become pregnant, contraception and timing are part of the conversation.
  • Certain gastrointestinal conditions such as severe gastroparesis, where slowed stomach emptying is already a problem.
  • Type 1 diabetes and specific medication combinations — insulin and sulfonylureas raise hypoglycemia risk and need dose management.

This is not a complete list, and none of it substitutes for a licensed clinician reviewing your history — which is precisely why a program that auto-approves everyone from a form, with no human screening these out, is doing something genuinely unsafe. Eligibility for a GLP-1 is a clinical decision, and no legitimate program can promise a prescription before that review. Insurance, Medicare and savings cards

Coverage is the single most confusing part of the branded-drug conversation, and it is a big reason compounded options became so popular. Here is the lay of the land, with the caveat that plans and policies change and you must verify your own.

Commercial insurance covers GLP-1s inconsistently for weight loss. Many plans cover them for type 2 diabetes but exclude or restrict them for weight management, and those that do cover weight-loss use often require prior authorization — documentation of BMI, prior attempts and sometimes a step through other options. Approvals are common for diabetes and far spottier for obesity alone. If your plan does cover a branded drug for weight loss, that is frequently the cheapest route of all, which is why a program with a real insurance-navigation service can be worth more than a rock-bottom compounded price.

Manufacturer savings cards can sharply lower the cost of branded Wegovy or Zepbound for eligible, commercially insured patients, and the makers have also introduced lower-cash-price self-pay options at times. These programs change, so check the manufacturer's current offer directly before assuming a price.

Medicare historically did not cover medications used solely for weight loss, which left many older adults paying cash or using compounded options. Coverage rules in this area have been evolving, and there are routes to the original branded drugs worth checking on medicare.gov rather than relying on a forum post. If you are on Medicare, confirm current coverage before defaulting to cash-pay compounded.

Compounded products are generally cash-pay and not run through insurance. That is part of why they are cheaper up front — you are not touching the prior-authorization machine — but it also means no insurance safety net. What happens when you stop taking a GLP-1?

This is the part the marketing most wants you to skip, and the part you should understand most clearly before you start. GLP-1s treat appetite while you take them; they do not permanently reset it. When the medication stops, the biological appetite signals it was suppressing tend to return — and for many people, so does weight.

The trial evidence is sobering. In a withdrawal extension of the semaglutide program, participants who stopped the drug regained a large share of the weight they had lost over the following year, with cardiometabolic improvements reversing in parallel. The lesson is not "these drugs don't work" — they plainly do — but that obesity behaves like a chronic condition, and stopping an effective treatment tends to bring the condition back, exactly as stopping blood-pressure medication tends to raise blood pressure again. Maintenance: staying on, stepping down, and the role of habits

Because stopping tends to bring weight back, "what's the long-term plan?" is the most important question most buyers never ask. There is no single right answer, but there are a few honest patterns.

Staying on a maintenance dose is, for many people, simply what treating a chronic condition looks like. Some can hold their results on a lower dose than the one that drove the loss; others need the full dose. This is a clinical decision, and it is a cost decision — which is why the maintenance price, not the intro price, is the number that determines whether a plan is sustainable for you over years, not months.

The evidence says this is hard and that regain is common, so go in with realistic expectations and a plan to resume if needed rather than treating a pause as a finish line.

The habits are not optional either way. The medication lowers the biological resistance that makes change so hard; what you build in that window — the protein habit, the strength base, the sleep, the relationship with food — is what gives you the best odds of keeping results whether you stay on, step down, or stop. Is a GLP-1 right for you? Realistic candidate profiles

These medications are powerful, but they are not for everyone, and matching the tool to the situation is part of using them well. A few honest profiles, drawn from how clinicians actually think about candidacy.

The clear candidate. An adult with obesity (commonly a BMI of 30 or more), or overweight with a weight-related condition such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol, prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, who has struggled to lose weight and keep it off through diet and activity alone. For this person, a GLP-1 is squarely within mainstream medical practice, and the trial evidence applies most directly. The main decisions are molecule, branded versus compounded, and which program.

The borderline candidate. Someone with a BMI in the upper-20s and no clear weight-related condition sits in a grayer zone. A GLP-1 may still be appropriate, but the risk-benefit math is closer, and a thoughtful clinician will weigh it carefully rather than wave it through. If a program approves this person instantly with no scrutiny, that is a sign the review was not real.

The person chasing a small cosmetic loss. Using a potent chronic medication, with real side effects and a lifelong cost, to drop the last ten vanity pounds is a poor trade for most people. The drug is designed for clinically meaningful weight that affects health, and the commitment it implies — likely long-term use — rarely fits a cosmetic goal. A good clinician will say so.

The person who cannot tolerate it, or should not take it. Some people get side effects they cannot live with even on careful titration; others have contraindications that take the drug off the table entirely. Neither is a failure. The point of a real evaluation is to find these out safely, and the point of a flexible program is to try the alternative molecule before concluding the whole class is unworkable.

Beyond the scale: the other health effects of GLP-1s

It is a mistake to think of these drugs as purely cosmetic. In the trials, weight loss came bundled with improvements in the markers that actually predict long, healthy life — and in some cases with hard outcome data.

Cardiovascular risk. The most striking evidence is the SELECT trial, in which semaglutide 2.4 mg cut the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (heart attack, stroke, cardiovascular death) by about 20% in people with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity but without diabetes. That moved the conversation from "weight-loss drug" to "cardiometabolic medicine," and it is a concrete reason the right candidate gains more than a smaller number on the scale.

Blood sugar and type 2 diabetes. Both molecules began as diabetes drugs and remain highly effective at lowering blood glucose and A1c. For people with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes, the metabolic benefit is central, not incidental.

Blood pressure, cholesterol and waist size. Across the weight-loss trials, participants generally saw reductions in blood pressure, improvements in lipid profiles and meaningful drops in waist circumference — the visceral fat that drives much of obesity's health harm.

Sleep apnea and other conditions. Tirzepatide has been studied specifically for obstructive sleep apnea in people with obesity, with meaningful improvements, and the class is being investigated across a range of obesity-related conditions. Many people also report better joint comfort, mobility and energy as weight comes off — second-order benefits that do not show up in a trial's primary endpoint but matter enormously day to day.

The flip side of all this is that these are real medicines with real physiological effects, which is the whole argument for taking them under genuine clinical supervision rather than buying unmonitored product from an anonymous site. The benefits and the risks come from the same potency.

The habits that make results last

The medication lowers the biological resistance that makes change so hard; what you build in that window decides how much of the result survives. This is not a motivational aside — it is the single biggest lever you control, and the programs worth paying for build support for it into the plan.

Protect your muscle. When you lose weight quickly, some of the loss can be muscle, not just fat — and muscle is metabolically precious and hard to rebuild. Two habits defend it: eating enough protein (a common target is roughly 0.6–0.8 grams per pound of goal body weight per day, adjusted to your clinician's advice), which is harder than it sounds when your appetite is suppressed, and resistance training two to four times a week. If you do one new thing while on a GLP-1, make it strength work. It is what separates "lost weight and looks healthy" from "lost weight and looks depleted."

Eat deliberately, not by accident. With appetite quiet, it becomes easy to under-eat protein and over-rely on whatever is convenient. Build meals around protein and vegetables, eat slowly (the slowed gastric emptying punishes speed with nausea), and stop at comfortably satisfied. The goal is not to white-knuckle a diet — the drug makes that unnecessary — but to make the smaller amount you eat count nutritionally.

Hydrate and mind the fiber. Constipation is a common complaint; fluids and fiber-rich foods help. Dehydration from GI side effects is a real early risk, so keep water close, especially after a dose increase.

Sleep and stress. Both drive appetite hormones and food reward independently of the drug. Poor sleep and high stress can blunt your results and your mood; protecting them is part of the treatment, not a luxury.

Build the habits while the drug is helping, not after. The window in which appetite is suppressed is the easiest time you will ever have to establish a protein habit, a training routine and a saner relationship with food. People who treat the medication as the whole plan tend to rebound; people who treat it as cover to rebuild their habits tend to keep more of what they gained. The drug buys you a fairer fight — spend the advantage wisely.

GLP-1s in specific situations

Candidacy is individual, and a few common situations deserve their own note because the standard advice bends around them. None of this replaces a clinician's judgment on your case.

PCOS and insulin resistance. Polycystic ovary syndrome is frequently tangled up with insulin resistance and weight that is unusually hard to shift. Many people with PCOS find GLP-1s helpful, and the metabolic effects can be particularly relevant — but if pregnancy is a possibility, timing matters, since these drugs are stopped before conception. Prediabetes. For someone with prediabetes and excess weight, a GLP-1 addresses both the weight and the glucose dysregulation at once, and the trial evidence for metabolic improvement is strong. This is often a textbook candidate, and the health upside extends well beyond the scale.

Very high starting weight. People with a high BMI often have the most to gain and frequently respond well, but they may also take longer to titrate and need a program that is genuinely supportive over a long arc. Older adults. Weight loss in older adults carries a sharper trade-off around muscle and bone, which makes protein intake and resistance training non-negotiable rather than optional. After bariatric surgery. GLP-1s are sometimes used alongside or after weight-loss surgery, including for regain, but this is a specialized scenario that belongs with a clinician who knows your surgical history — not a generic intake form.

People on multiple medications. Because GLP-1s slow gastric emptying and can affect blood sugar, interactions matter — especially with insulin, sulfonylureas and some oral medications whose absorption timing is sensitive. How GLP-1s compare to other weight-loss options

GLP-1s are not the only tool, and honest guidance places them in context rather than pretending they are the only path.

Lifestyle alone (diet and exercise). The foundation of any plan and genuinely sufficient for some people — but for many with obesity, biology pushes the weight back no matter how disciplined the effort, which is the whole reason this drug class matters. GLP-1s do not replace lifestyle change; they make it feasible where it was not. The habits still do essential work.

Older weight-loss medications. Options such as phentermine (short-term), the combination drugs naltrexone-bupropion and phentermine-topiramate, and the older daily GLP-1 liraglutide exist and help some people, but they generally produce less weight loss than semaglutide or tirzepatide and carry their own side-effect profiles. They are still worth discussing if the newer drugs are unaffordable or poorly tolerated.

Bariatric surgery. For people with severe obesity, surgery remains the most powerful and durable intervention, with its own risks, permanence and recovery. GLP-1s have narrowed the gap for many, and the choice between an injection and an operation is a serious, individual conversation — not an either/or the internet can settle for you.

Over-the-counter "GLP-1 boosters" and supplements. Be skeptical. Supplements marketed as natural GLP-1 boosters are not the same as the prescription medications and do not produce comparable results; the evidence is thin and the marketing is loud. If a product promises Ozempic-like results without a prescription, that is a reason to close the tab.

Seen in context, GLP-1s are the most effective pharmacological option available for most people, sitting between lifestyle change (which they amplify rather than replace) and surgery (which remains more powerful for the most severe cases). For the large middle of people who have tried to lose weight and watched biology undo it, they are, for now, the best tool medicine offers — provided you buy them the way this guide describes.

The evidence base, in more detail

Because this is a decision about your health, it is worth seeing where the headline numbers come from rather than trusting a marketing slide. The modern GLP-1 weight-loss story rests on three bodies of randomized, placebo-controlled evidence published in the New England Journal of Medicine, plus the regulatory record.

The STEP program (semaglutide). STEP 1 is the anchor trial for semaglutide 2.4 mg in people with overweight or obesity: about 14.9% average weight loss over 68 weeks, with roughly 86% of participants losing at least 5% and around a third losing 20% or more (Wilding et al., 2021). Its companion trials extended the picture — semaglutide in people with type 2 diabetes, in combination with intensive behavioral therapy, and across continued-versus-withdrawn treatment — and it was a withdrawal arm that produced the sobering regain-after-stopping data this guide keeps returning to. Taken together, the STEP program is why semaglutide is an established, evidence-backed obesity treatment rather than an off-label experiment.

The SURMOUNT program (tirzepatide). SURMOUNT-1 is the anchor for tirzepatide: average weight reductions of about 15%, 19.5% and 20.9% at the 5, 10 and 15 mg doses over 72 weeks, with more than nine in ten participants on the top dose losing at least 5% (Jastreboff et al., 2022). Later SURMOUNT trials studied tirzepatide in people with type 2 diabetes, in maintenance, and — importantly for anyone weighing the two drugs — head-to-head against semaglutide, where tirzepatide produced greater average weight loss. That head-to-head result is the strongest single piece of evidence behind tirzepatide's "more effective on average" reputation, and it is why we frame the molecule choice the way we do.

The SELECT trial (cardiovascular outcomes). SELECT took semaglutide 2.4 mg into harder territory: a large trial in people with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity but without diabetes, measuring not weight but heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular death. Semaglutide reduced those major events by about 20% (Lincoff et al., 2023). This is the result that reclassified the drug in many clinicians' minds from "weight-loss medication" to "cardiometabolic therapy," and it is a concrete reason the right candidate should want a real clinical evaluation rather than a transactional one.

The regulatory record. Alongside the trials sits the FDA's paper trail: the 2021 approval of Wegovy, the 2023 approval of Zepbound, the drug-level patient information on MedlinePlus, and the agency's warnings about unapproved GLP-1 products and compounding dosing errors. That record is what lets us say clearly which products are FDA-approved and which are not — the distinction that the compounded market works hard to blur.

Two honest limits round this out. First, trial populations are selected and supported in ways the real world is not, so real-world results and adherence run below trial averages — the gap we covered under real-world adherence. Second, the newest comparisons and the long-term outcome picture are still filling in, and the compounded-product evidence base is far thinner than the branded one because those products were never required to run the trials. None of that undoes the core finding — these drugs work, powerfully — but it argues for humility, clinical supervision, and a plan that treats this as long-term care.

GLP-1 weight loss: frequently asked questions

What is the most effective GLP-1 for weight loss?

On the pivotal trial averages, tirzepatide (Zepbound) produced the most weight loss — roughly 21% at the top 15 mg dose over 72 weeks — compared with about 15% for semaglutide (Wegovy) over 68 weeks. That makes tirzepatide the statistically strongest starting point for most people. But "most effective" is an average: many people do excellent on semaglutide, tolerance varies between the two, and the best choice also depends on cost, cardiovascular history and availability. The molecule you can stay on comfortably and afford beats the one with the bigger headline you quit.

Can I get a GLP-1 online without insurance?

Yes. Most people who buy GLP-1s through telehealth pay cash, especially for compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide, which typically run around $109–$169 a month at a maintenance dose. Branded Wegovy or Zepbound without insurance usually run around $300+ a month cash, though manufacturer savings options can lower that. You still need a legitimate clinician evaluation — "without insurance" does not mean "without a prescription."

Is compounded semaglutide safe?

Compounded semaglutide is not FDA-approved, which means its safety, effectiveness and quality are not verified by the agency before it reaches you. It can be a reasonable, far cheaper option when it comes from a transparent program working with a reputable, named pharmacy (ideally a 503B outsourcing facility), but the FDA has warned about dosing errors and about products using non-standard salt forms. The risk is real but manageable: insist on a named pharmacy, clear dosing instructions or pre-filled pens, and a real clinician. Avoid anything sold as "research use only."

How much does a GLP-1 cost per month without insurance?

Plan around the maintenance price, not the intro teaser. Compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide typically costs about $138 a month on average (most land $109–$169); branded Wegovy or Zepbound without insurance typically runs around $335 a month and can be higher. The "$39 first month" ads are intro rates that climb once you titrate up or a membership fee is added. Over a year, the maintenance price times twelve — plus any prepaid bundle — is the number that decides value.

Do I need a prescription for semaglutide or tirzepatide?

Yes, always. Both are prescription medications, and any legitimate program requires a licensed clinician to review your health history before prescribing. A service that offers them with no medical evaluation, or "guarantees" a prescription up front, is not one to trust. The evaluation is also where contraindications — like a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer — get screened out, which is why it is not a formality.

How quickly will I lose weight on a GLP-1?

Slowly at first, by design. Because you start at a low dose and step up over weeks (titration), early loss is usually a few pounds in the first month — appetite changes tend to show up before big scale changes. The rate typically picks up as you reach effective doses around months two to four, then tapers toward a plateau over the following months. Expecting dramatic month-one results is the main reason people quit a drug that was working.

Will I regain the weight if I stop?

Often, yes, without a plan. GLP-1s suppress appetite while you take them; they do not permanently reset it. Trial withdrawal data showed participants regained a large share of lost weight in the year after stopping, with metabolic improvements reversing too. Obesity behaves like a chronic condition, so stopping an effective treatment tends to bring it back — much like stopping blood-pressure medication. The best odds of keeping results come from a maintenance plan and the habits (protein, strength training, sleep) you build while on the drug.

What's the difference between semaglutide, Ozempic and Wegovy?

Semaglutide is the molecule. Ozempic is the brand approved for type 2 diabetes; Wegovy is the brand approved for weight management — same active ingredient, studied and dosed for weight loss. Compounded semaglutide is the same molecule prepared by a compounding pharmacy rather than the manufacturer, and is not FDA-approved. When an ad says "semaglutide," it usually means the compounded or generic-molecule version; "Wegovy" specifically means the branded weight-loss product.

What's the difference between tirzepatide, Mounjaro and Zepbound?

Same pattern. Tirzepatide is the molecule (a dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist). Mounjaro is the brand approved for type 2 diabetes; Zepbound is the brand approved for weight management. Compounded tirzepatide is the same molecule from a compounding pharmacy and is not FDA-approved. All deliver tirzepatide; the brand, approval status and price differ.

Can I switch between semaglutide and tirzepatide?

Yes, with a clinician's guidance, and the ability to switch is a genuinely useful feature in a program. People discover their tolerance and their budget by living with a drug, not by reading about it — so a program that can move you between molecules (and between compounded and branded) without making you start the process over can be the difference between staying on treatment and giving up. We weight this flexibility heavily in our scoring.

What are the most common side effects?

Gastrointestinal: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation and reflux, concentrated in the early weeks and after each dose increase. In the STEP 1 trial about 44% of participants reported nausea at some point, usually mild to moderate and fading with time. Less common but important are gallbladder problems, rare pancreatitis, dehydration and (with insulin or sulfonylureas) low blood sugar. Smaller meals, hydration and a patient titration handle most of it; persistent severe abdominal pain needs urgent care.

Who should not take a GLP-1?

People with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2 (a boxed-warning contraindication), and those who are pregnant or trying to conceive, generally should not. A history of pancreatitis, severe gastroparesis, type 1 diabetes, or certain medication combinations require careful clinician judgment. This is not a complete list, which is exactly why a real medical evaluation — not an auto-approval — is essential before starting.

How do I know if an online clinic is legit?

Run five checks: it is licensed to prescribe in your state; it names its pharmacy and type (503A or 503B); a licensed clinician actually reviews your intake; you can cancel online; and refund terms are in writing. Red flags: guaranteed prescriptions, "research-only" peptides, a hidden pharmacy, no way to reach a human about side effects, and high-pressure prepaid plans with no refund policy. Legitimate medicine does not work on guarantees.

What's the difference between a 503A and 503B pharmacy?

A 503A pharmacy compounds for an individual patient against a specific prescription. A 503B outsourcing facility registers with the FDA, is inspected to tighter, manufacturer-adjacent standards, and can make larger batches. Neither produces an FDA-approved drug, but 503B generally operates under more oversight. A program that names its pharmacy and type is being transparent; one that will not is a red flag.

Does insurance cover GLP-1s for weight loss?

Inconsistently. Many commercial plans cover them for type 2 diabetes but restrict or exclude them for weight loss, and plans that do cover weight-loss use often require prior authorization. If your plan does cover a branded drug for weight management, that is frequently the cheapest route — which is why a program with real insurance navigation can be worth more than the lowest compounded price. Always verify your own plan's current policy.

Does Medicare cover GLP-1s?

Historically Medicare did not cover drugs used solely for weight loss, which pushed many older adults toward cash-pay or compounded options. The rules in this area have been changing, and there are routes to the original branded drugs worth confirming directly on medicare.gov rather than trusting a forum post. If you are on Medicare, check current coverage before defaulting to cash-pay compounded.

Are GLP-1 pills as effective as injections?

The strongest weight-loss evidence is for the once-weekly injectables used in the trials. Oral options exist — an approved oral semaglutide for diabetes and compounded sublingual or troche formats from some telehealth programs — but compounded oral products are less studied than the injectables, and you should not assume they match the trial results. If needle avoidance is your reason, discuss it with a clinician rather than assuming equivalence.

What BMI do you need to qualify?

Eligibility is a clinical decision, but these medications are generally considered for adults with a higher body-mass index (commonly a BMI of 30 or above), or a somewhat lower BMI (around 27+) alongside a weight-related condition such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol or type 2 diabetes. A clinician weighs your full history, not just the number. No legitimate program can promise a prescription before that review.

Can I drink alcohol on a GLP-1?

There is no absolute prohibition, but moderation is wise. Alcohol can irritate the stomach and worsen nausea, especially early in titration, and it adds empty calories that work against your goal. Interestingly, some people report reduced interest in alcohol on these drugs, a reflection of their effect on reward pathways. Follow your clinician's guidance, particularly if you have liver concerns or take other medications.

What happens if I miss a dose?

For the once-weekly injectables, guidance generally allows taking a missed dose within a few days of the scheduled time, and otherwise skipping it and resuming your normal schedule — but follow the specific product instructions and your prescriber's advice, as the exact window differs by drug. Do not double up to "catch up." If you miss several weeks, talk to your program, since you may need to step the dose back down to limit side effects on restarting.

How long do people stay on GLP-1s?

Because weight tends to return after stopping, many people treat GLP-1s as a long-term, even indefinite, therapy for a chronic condition — sometimes at a lower maintenance dose once results are reached. Others attempt a supervised step-down paired with strong habits, accepting that regain is common and that resuming is an option. There is no fixed course length; the honest framing is "as long as the benefit outweighs the cost and burden," which is a decision for you and your clinician.

What's the cheapest way to get a GLP-1 legitimately?

For most cash-pay buyers, compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide from a transparent, reputable telehealth program is the lowest real monthly cost. The cheapest flat rates we track start around $99/mo for compounded semaglutide — for example, Eden, which holds that price at every dose with no membership — and most compounded programs land roughly $99–$169 at a maintenance dose. If your insurance covers a branded drug for weight loss, that can be cheaper still. Either way, compare on the maintenance price (not the intro teaser), avoid prepaid bundles with no refund terms, and never trade a slightly lower price for an anonymous pharmacy or a program with no clinician support.

Can I take a GLP-1 if I don't have diabetes?

Yes. Semaglutide (Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Zepbound) are FDA-approved specifically for weight management in people with overweight or obesity who do not have diabetes, and the pivotal trials were run in that population. You do not need diabetes to be a candidate; you need a qualifying BMI (or a lower BMI with a weight-related condition) and a clinician's judgment that the benefits outweigh the risks for you.

What is "Ozempic face," and should I worry about it?

"Ozempic face" is a nickname for the facial hollowing or sagging some people notice after rapid weight loss. It is not a special effect of the drug — it is what losing fat quickly does to the face, the same as any rapid weight loss. The ways to limit it are the same habits that protect the rest of your body: lose at a sustainable pace, eat enough protein, and do resistance training to preserve muscle and overall tone. Faster is not better, and this is one more reason to choose a sane, clinician-paced titration over a crash.

Will I have loose skin after losing weight on a GLP-1?

Significant weight loss by any method can leave loose skin, and GLP-1s are no exception — the larger the loss and the faster it happens, the more likely it is. Skin elasticity, age and genetics all play a role. A gradual pace, strength training to fill the frame with muscle, good hydration and protein all help; for very large losses, some people eventually consider cosmetic procedures, but that is a later and separate decision. It is not a reason to avoid treatment, just a reason to lose weight at a healthy rate.

How is compounded GLP-1 legal if it isn't FDA-approved?

Compounding is a separate, long-standing legal category from drug approval. Pharmacies are permitted to compound medications under specific conditions — historically including when an approved drug is in shortage, which is how the large compounded-GLP-1 market grew during the 2022–2024 shortages. "Not FDA-approved" means the agency has not reviewed that specific compounded product's safety, effectiveness and quality — not necessarily that selling it is illegal. The legal footing has been narrowing as shortages resolved, which is why it is a shifting area and why a program's ability to move you to a branded product matters. When in doubt, the FDA's compounding Q&A is the authoritative reference.

Common myths about GLP-1 weight loss, corrected

The space is thick with half-truths. A few worth clearing up, because believing them leads to bad decisions.

"It's the easy way out / cheating." Obesity is a chronic disease driven substantially by biology, and GLP-1s treat that biology the way other medicines treat other chronic conditions. Nobody calls insulin or statins "cheating." The drug lowers the biological resistance; you still do the work of building habits around it.

"You take it for a few months and you're done." This is the single most expensive myth. Weight tends to return after stopping, so these are generally long-term treatments. A program selling a short "course" is selling you a rebound. Plan for maintenance from day one.

"Compounded is the same as the brand, just cheaper." Same molecule, different regulatory category. Compounded products are not FDA-approved, quality depends on the pharmacy, and the legal ground has been shifting. Cheaper can be perfectly reasonable — but it is not identical, and transparency about the pharmacy matters.

"The '$39/month' ad is what I'll pay." Almost never. That is an intro rate; the maintenance price is usually two to five times higher once you titrate up or a membership fee is added. Always shop on the month-four number.

"More weight loss is always better / faster is better." Rapid loss raises gallstone and muscle-loss risk, and a dose you cannot tolerate is a dose you quit. Sustainable, clinician-paced loss that you can maintain beats a crash you abandon. The best result is the one you keep.

"If I just had more willpower I wouldn't need this." Willpower fights a biological signal that these drugs quiet directly. People who could never sustain a diet often find change feasible for the first time precisely because the medication changes the terrain — not because they suddenly became more disciplined.

A quick glossary of the terms you'll see

  • GLP-1 receptor agonist — a medication that mimics the gut hormone GLP-1 to reduce appetite and slow stomach emptying. Semaglutide is one.
  • Dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist — a medication (tirzepatide) that acts on two gut-hormone receptors, associated with greater average weight loss in trials.
  • Semaglutide — the molecule in Wegovy (weight loss) and Ozempic (diabetes).
  • Tirzepatide — the molecule in Zepbound (weight loss) and Mounjaro (diabetes).
  • Branded — the FDA-approved manufacturer product (Wegovy, Zepbound). Tested, consistent, costlier.
  • Compounded — the same molecule prepared by a compounding pharmacy; not FDA-approved; cheaper; quality depends on the pharmacy.
  • 503A / 503B — categories of compounding pharmacy; 503B outsourcing facilities operate under tighter, FDA-registered oversight.
  • Titration — the gradual dose increase over weeks that limits side effects.
  • Maintenance dose — the ongoing dose you settle on; its price is the one that determines affordability.
  • Prior authorization — insurer paperwork required before a branded drug is covered, common for weight-loss use.
  • Intro vs. maintenance price — the teaser you are shown vs. the real monthly cost you keep paying.

The bottom line

GLP-1 medications are the most effective weight-loss treatment medicine has produced, and for most people, getting them online is now a legitimate, mainstream path. The science is strong: roughly 15% average weight loss on semaglutide and up to about 21% on tirzepatide in the pivotal trials, with real improvements in health markers and, for semaglutide, hard cardiovascular evidence. The pitfalls are not medical so much as commercial — teaser pricing that hides the real cost, anonymous pharmacies, prepay traps, and programs that sell a start without a plan for the long arc these medications actually require.

So approach it like the long-term health decision it is. Choose on the maintenance price, not the intro rate. Insist on a named pharmacy, a real clinician and a clean cancellation path. Prefer a program flexible enough to switch your molecule or move you between compounded and branded as your tolerance and budget reveal themselves. Build the protein, strength and sleep habits that make the results durable. And treat the exit as seriously as the entrance, because that is where results are kept or lost.
